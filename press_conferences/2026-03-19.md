# BOJ Press Conference — 18–19 Mar 2026

**Decision:** Hold at 0.75%  
**Vote:** 8–1 (Takata dissented for hike)  
**Presider:** Governor UEDA Kazuo

---

The BOJ judged that Japan’s economy is recovering moderately, although some
weak signs remain.

Looking ahead, overseas economies are expected to return to a growth path
despite the impact of trade policies and other factors. Supported by
government economic measures and accommodative financial conditions,
Japan’s positive cycle from income to spending is expected to strengthen
gradually, allowing the economy to continue growing moderately.

However, Middle East tensions have led to unstable moves in global
financial markets, and crude oil prices have risen sharply. These
developments require close attention.

On prices, CPI excluding fresh food has remained above 2%, reflecting
continued pass-through of wage increases to sales prices and higher food
prices such as rice. Recently, however, it has declined to around 2% due
partly to government energy support measures.

Looking ahead, CPI excluding fresh food is expected to temporarily fall
below 2% as food-price effects fade and government measures weigh on
inflation. After that, higher crude oil prices are expected to push
inflation up again.

The wage-price mechanism is expected to remain in place. As the economy
continues to improve, labor shortages should intensify, medium- to
long-term inflation expectations should rise, and underlying inflation
should gradually increase. In the second half of the Outlook Report
projection period, underlying inflation is expected to move at levels
broadly consistent with the 2% price stability target.

Given that real interest rates remain extremely low, if this outlook
materializes, the BOJ expects to continue raising the policy rate and
adjusting the degree of monetary accommodation in line with improvements in
economic and price conditions.

The BOJ also said it will improve communication on underlying inflation.
Because government measures and oil-price movements may make CPI more
volatile and harder to interpret, the BOJ plans to expand publication of
CPI-related indicators and explain underlying inflation more carefully. It
is also re-estimating Japan’s potential growth rate, output gap, and
possibly the neutral rate following the GDP benchmark revision.


## Q&A Transcript

### Q1. How do Middle East tensions affect Japan’s economy and prices? In policy terms, should the BOJ focus more on downside growth risks or upside inflation risks?

**A:**

At today’s meeting, we maintained our central outlook: Japan’s economy
should continue growing moderately, and underlying inflation should reach
levels consistent with the 2% target in the second half of the projection
period.

However, if crude oil prices continue to rise, they could weigh on the
economy through worsening terms of trade. At the same time, higher oil
prices could lift energy prices and, through higher inflation expectations,
push up underlying inflation.

If such a situation occurs, it is difficult to say in advance whether
policy should place more weight on inflation control or supporting the
economy. We will judge based on the size of the inflationary and economic
effects, and ultimately choose the response most appropriate for achieving
the 2% price stability target in a sustainable and stable manner.

---

### Q2. What will the BOJ focus on when deciding the timing of the next rate hike?

**A:**

As before, the BOJ will decide at each meeting by assessing the outlook for
economic activity, prices, and underlying inflation, as well as the risks
around that outlook.

For now, a key point is how Middle East tensions affect Japan’s economy.
Before the recent deterioration in the Middle East, recent data showed
solid developments in both income and spending by households and firms.
Government economic measures are also expected to support growth.

We will examine how much higher oil prices and worsening terms of trade may
weigh on activity.

For underlying inflation, risks can move in both directions. If the economy
is pushed down and the output gap worsens, that would weigh on underlying
inflation. But if higher oil prices or yen depreciation raise medium- to
long-term inflation expectations, that would push underlying inflation
higher.

We will closely watch spring wage negotiations, corporate price-setting
behavior, and whether the wage-price mechanism continues to operate. We
will also analyze branch-level hearing information carefully.

---

### Q3. What lessons does the BOJ draw from past oil shocks and from the 2021–22 experience in the US and Europe?

**A:**

We have reviewed past episodes, including Japan’s 1970s oil shocks and the
2021–22 experience in the US and Europe.

These episodes are useful references, but our basic approach remains the
same: we will choose the most appropriate policy response from the
perspective of achieving the 2% price stability target in a sustainable and
stable manner.

---

### Q4. Is the BOJ’s dialogue with the government working well, especially given appointments of reflationist board members?

**A:**

I will refrain from commenting on board appointments while the nomination
process is ongoing.

On dialogue with the government, we have worked hard to exchange views
closely, and I believe this has been functioning well. We will continue
close communication.

---

### Q5. Does an oil-price shock become a reason for rate hikes if it affects underlying inflation?

**A:**

A temporary supply shock is generally something monetary policy should look
through.

However, it is difficult to judge in advance whether a shock is truly
temporary or how long it will affect underlying inflation. Therefore, at
each meeting, we will make the best possible judgment based on the
information available at that time.

Even without Middle East tensions, our baseline outlook is that underlying
inflation gradually rises and reaches 2% in the second half of the
projection period. We will assess whether the new shock pushes that path
upward or downward.

---

### Q6. How does yen depreciation affect the BOJ’s policy judgment?

**A:**

I will not comment on specific exchange-rate movements.

However, we will carefully monitor how exchange-rate movements affect
prices and underlying inflation. Compared with the past, exchange-rate
pass-through to domestic prices may have become stronger, and the effect on
underlying inflation may also be larger. That requires close attention.

---

### Q7. Do board members now place more weight on upside inflation risks?

**A:**

At today’s meeting, members discussed this point extensively.

Some members emphasized upside risks to underlying inflation, while others
emphasized downside risks. My impression is that slightly more members
focused on upside inflation risks.

---

### Q8. How does the BOJ view the impact of Middle East tensions on the global economy?

**A:**

Higher oil prices create inflationary pressure globally. They also weigh on
economic activity through worsening terms of trade, especially through
higher energy and raw-material costs.

The impact depends on how long the Middle East situation lasts. We also
need to consider whether shipping and logistics disruptions could constrain
supply chains, and whether these developments could damage corporate and
consumer sentiment.

---

### Q9. Does the Governor personally see upside or downside risks to underlying inflation as larger?

**A:**

At this stage, I cannot clearly say which is larger.

The situation has changed rapidly since March and continues to change day
by day. I would like to observe developments further before judging which
risk is more important.

That said, if upside risks to underlying inflation become significant,
policy could be considered from a risk-management perspective, even if the
central outlook itself is still being assessed.

---

### Q10. What information will the BOJ look for in the next Tankan survey?

**A:**

We want to understand both the underlying strength of the economy so far
and the additional impact of Middle East tensions.

However, depending on the timing of survey responses, the Tankan may not
fully capture the impact of recent Middle East developments. We will look
at the distribution and timing of responses before judging how useful it is.

---

### Q11. How should Taylor-rule thinking apply to Japan’s current policy setting?

**A:**

If one simply plugs current inflation into a Taylor rule, it would likely
imply a policy rate much higher than the current rate.

But because monetary policy works with lags, the relevant input should not
be just current inflation. It should be forward-looking inflation, and
ultimately something closer to underlying inflation.

In that sense, Taylor-rule thinking is not very different from what the BOJ
is already doing: assessing underlying inflation and the outlook.

---

### Q12. Is it difficult for markets to price Japan’s inflation path correctly?

**A:**

Yes. When inflation rises sharply, it is difficult for markets to judge how
persistent the rise will be and how the central bank will respond.

This partly reflects the difficulty of judging underlying inflation itself.
We have struggled with communication on this point. That is why we plan to
expand indicators and improve communication, so that the BOJ and markets
can develop a more shared understanding of underlying inflation.

---

### Q13. How does the BOJ assess the latest spring wage negotiations?

**A:**

The information so far suggests wage negotiations are becoming solid.

The key question is how far wage increases spread to small and medium-sized
firms. Before the results, there were many indications that wage increases
could broaden more than usual. We will examine upcoming Rengo data and our
own hearing information carefully.

---

### Q14. Does the government understand the difference between headline inflation and underlying inflation?

**A:**

My impression is that the government understands the importance of
something similar to underlying inflation, even if it does not always use
that term.

The government also understands the difference between headline inflation
and underlying inflation. I do not feel there is a gap between the BOJ and
the government on this point.

---

### Q15. Can the BOJ judge the impact of an oil shock on underlying inflation within one or two months?

**A:**

It is difficult to make a precise judgment in a short period.

However, underlying inflation is assessed by accumulating a wide range of
data. If a new shock occurs, we can assess how much it changes our previous
view. While precise judgment is difficult, it may be possible even within a
short period to judge the likely direction and approximate size of the
impact.

---

### Q16. What would count as second-round effects from higher oil prices?

**A:**

A strong second-round effect would involve broad-based price increases
across many items, followed by spillovers to wages and then back into
prices.

How early we can identify such a process depends on the situation.
Sometimes it may be visible early; in other cases, we may need to wait for
more data.

---

### Q17. Can the BOJ hike rates while financial markets are unstable?

**A:**

We are monitoring global financial markets daily. Risk sentiment has
weakened somewhat, and we will take that into account.

In general, financial-market developments affect policy through their
impact on the economy and prices. We also need to check whether a large
shock is creating financial-system instability. At present, we do not see
any systemic financial problem.

---

### Q18. Is Japan facing stagflation risk?

**A:**

It depends on how one defines stagflation.

Other things equal, higher oil prices push inflation up and put downward
pressure on growth. That is true. But the size and duration of those
effects are highly uncertain.

---

### Q19. Why did the BOJ hold rates if underlying inflation is approaching 2% and wage data look strong?

**A:**

We did not change the central outlook, but the probability that this
outlook will be realized has declined somewhat.

At the same time, the probability of risk scenarios has increased,
especially the new risk scenario associated with higher oil prices. That is
why we maintained policy today.

By the next meeting, more data and information will be available. We will
reassess the outlook and risks, then make an appropriate policy decision.

---

### Q20. Could yen weakness itself increase inflation risk if the BOJ focuses too much on downside growth risks?

**A:**

I will not comment on how the exchange-rate market may move.

However, we will closely monitor the impact of exchange-rate movements on
the economy and prices, especially on inflation and underlying inflation.
Exchange-rate pass-through to domestic prices may now be stronger than in
the past, and its impact on underlying inflation may also be larger.

---

### Q21. Is the new publication of underlying inflation indicators intended to make rate hikes easier to explain?

**A:**

No. The purpose is not to steer policy in one direction.

Underlying inflation is a difficult concept and must be judged
comprehensively using many indicators. We want to publish more useful
indicators and discuss them with the public and markets, so that our
assessment of underlying inflation becomes easier to understand.

Following the GDP benchmark revision, we are also re-estimating the output
gap and neutral rate. We will consider publishing the results once they are
ready.

---

### Q22. How is Deputy Governor Uchida’s health affecting BOJ operations?

**A:**

Day-to-day work is being handled through phone, email, and telework without
major difficulty.

To avoid infection risk, he participated remotely in today’s meeting. We
are waiting for doctors’ judgment on when he can physically return.

---

### Q23. If growth slows temporarily due to oil prices, can the BOJ still hike?

**A:**

Yes, a rate hike is possible if the slowdown is temporary and does not
significantly affect the path of underlying inflation.

The wording in the current statement may be somewhat hard to understand, so
we will reconsider how to explain it in the April statement, together with
the updated outlook.

---

### Q24. Does the BOJ coordinate policy with other central banks in response to global oil shocks?

**A:**

No. Each central bank decides what it considers optimal for its own economy.

Compared with four years ago, Japan’s underlying inflation is now closer to
2%, whereas at that time it was still very low and stable. Other economies
started from a higher underlying inflation position. Today, many other
central banks have underlying inflation around target, while Japan is
approaching 2% but still has some distance to go.

---

### Q25. Is a rate hike included among the possible decisions at the next meeting?

**A:**

At every meeting, we examine the information available up to that point and
make the appropriate policy decision. That includes assessing whether
adjustment of monetary accommodation is appropriate.

---

### Q26. Is Japan’s economy resilient enough to absorb this shock, or could it return to deflation?

**A:**

It depends on the size and persistence of the shock.

Underlying inflation is approaching 2%, but it is not yet firmly anchored.
We need to carefully assess whether Japan can truly anchor inflation around
2% despite this shock, and avoid making a policy mistake.
