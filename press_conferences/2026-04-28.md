# BOJ Press Conference — 27–28 Apr 2026

**Decision:** Hold at 0.75%  
**Vote:** 6–3 (Nakagawa, Takata, Tamura dissented for hike)  
**Presider:** Governor UEDA Kazuo

---

The BOJ kept the uncollateralized overnight call rate target at around
*0.75%* by majority vote.

Three board members — *Nakagawa, Takata, and Tamura* — dissented and
proposed raising the policy rate to around *1.0%*, citing upside inflation
risks, the achievement of the price stability target, and the need to move
closer to neutral rates.

The BOJ’s baseline outlook assumes Middle East tensions gradually ease,
crude oil prices decline from around *USD105/bbl* toward the *USD70s*, and
no major supply-chain disruption occurs.

The BOJ sees Japan’s economy continuing to recover moderately, although
growth in FY2026 is revised down due to higher oil prices and worsening
terms of trade. Inflation is revised up sharply for FY2026, mainly due to
oil and related pass-through effects.

Underlying inflation is expected to approach levels consistent with the 2%
target from the second half of FY2026 into FY2027.

The BOJ will continue raising rates and adjusting the degree of monetary
accommodation depending on economic, price, and financial conditions.


## Q&A Transcript

### Q1. How do you assess the two-sided risks from Middle East tensions — downside growth risk and upside inflation risk? Are you behind the curve by keeping rates unchanged?

**A:**

For FY2026, we judge that downside risks to growth and upside risks to
prices are both large.

On growth, if Middle East tensions persist and oil prices remain high,
Japan could face further downward pressure through worsening terms of trade
and supply-chain effects. Japan has some resilience from strong corporate
profits and government support measures, but the economy could slow more
depending on the size and persistence of the shock.

On prices, although slower growth would put downward pressure on underlying
inflation through the output gap, overall we see upside inflation risks as
larger. Oil is used widely across industries, so higher oil prices can push
up not only energy prices but also a broad range of goods prices. Given
that firms are now more active in wage- and price-setting than in the past,
oil-price increases may pass through more easily to goods and services
prices, and could also lift inflation expectations.

We are not ignoring the risk of falling behind the curve. Underlying
inflation is approaching 2%, and firms are becoming more active in raising
prices, especially petroleum-related products. We will carefully examine
data and information and make appropriate decisions at future meetings so
that we do not fall behind the curve.

---

### Q2. The FY2027–28 core-core CPI forecasts remain well above 2%. Does this imply near-term rate hikes and several further hikes toward neutral? Are inflation expectations anchored?

**A:**

Core and core-core inflation forecasts have been revised up significantly
from the previous Outlook Report. This mainly reflects the pass-through of
higher oil prices to various goods and, in some cases, services. Our
baseline view is that this effect is temporary, although the duration may
vary depending on which price measure one looks at.

Our view on underlying inflation has not changed: it is currently slightly
below 2%, but is expected to rise gradually and reach around 2% from the
second half of FY2026 into FY2027, then remain around that level.

That said, we are very aware of the risk that higher headline inflation
could push up underlying inflation. Medium- to long-term inflation
expectations are approaching 2%, but they are not fully anchored at 2%.
Therefore, expectations could still move either upward or downward, and we
need to monitor this carefully.

---

### Q3. If inflation forecasts were revised up, why did the BOJ still hold rates? Are growth downside risks and inflation upside risks roughly balanced?

**A:**

The basic reason for holding rates is that confidence in the baseline
outlook has declined.

The risk scenario involves both upside inflation risk and downside growth
risk. On growth, we want to assess whether a major downturn could occur. On
prices, we want to assess whether underlying inflation could clearly
overshoot. We need to confirm these developments further before deciding.

There is no preset number of months needed for this assessment. At future
meetings, including the next one, we will examine the likelihood of the
baseline outlook and the nature of the risks before making an appropriate
decision.

---

### Q4. Three board members dissented and called for a hike. How do you view this? Does it affect BOJ credibility?

**A:**

As chair, I take the fact that three members dissented seriously.

That said, the split reflects how difficult policy judgment is under a
negative supply shock, where growth is revised down while inflation is
revised up.

The dissenting members were concerned about upside inflation risk and
believed it was appropriate to raise rates now to prepare for that risk.
The remaining six members also recognize inflation upside risks, but judged
that there was not yet enough urgency to respond immediately with a rate
hike. They wanted to assess how the risks and the baseline outlook evolve.

For a temporary supply shock, it can be appropriate to look through the
first-round inflation effect. But if second-round effects emerge and affect
underlying inflation, we would need to respond appropriately through rate
hikes.

---

### Q5. Are you now considering rate hikes specifically to address upside inflation risk? Will the BOJ send clear signals before future hikes?

**A:**

Yes, the possibility of raising rates in response to upside inflation risk
is something we are considering. But it would not necessarily be the only
reason for future rate hikes.

On communication, we will continue to consider what form of communication
is appropriate, taking account of current economic and financial conditions.

---

### Q6. Earlier you said that if inflation risks increase, the BOJ may need to adjust rates earlier. Why did that not lead to action today?

**A:**

Headline inflation is likely to rise somewhat significantly in the near
term. That does not immediately mean underlying inflation will rise. But
because wage- and price-setting behavior has become more active, we must
watch the risk that medium- to long-term inflation expectations rise
clearly and underlying inflation overshoots.

We want to act appropriately so that does not happen.

Regarding the policy review, one lesson was that until around 2021–22,
inflation and wage norms were anchored near zero, and it was very difficult
to escape that. Japan has now moved some distance away from that situation,
and medium- to long-term inflation expectations are approaching 2%. But we
are not yet confident they are anchored at 2%. Therefore, policy should aim
to anchor them around 2% as much as possible.

---

### Q7. If downside risks to the economy become larger, how would policy respond?

**A:**

It is not clear whether price risks or growth risks would materialize first.

Energy and related goods prices will rise more visibly, but whether this
affects underlying inflation is less clear. At the same time, if
supply-chain problems spread, the impact on the economy could also appear
relatively quickly.

We will not prejudge the outcome. We will adjust monetary policy
appropriately depending on how risks materialize.

---

### Q8. How far is the current policy rate from neutral? Is the BOJ in a hurry to hike?

**A:**

Estimating neutral rates through data analysis has significant limitations.

Rather than relying solely on estimates, we assess after each rate hike how
financial and economic conditions evolve and whether financial conditions
remain accommodative.

Since the December hike, real interest rates remain negative, especially
from short- to medium-term maturities. Lending continues to increase,
banks’ lending attitudes remain accommodative, and CP market conditions
remain favorable. Therefore, we judge that financial conditions are still
accommodative.

---

### Q9. Are second-round effects from higher oil prices already visible? What would justify a hike even if confidence in the baseline outlook remains low?

**A:**

At present, we do not think medium- to long-term inflation expectations
have jumped sharply. Therefore, while this is a risk we need to watch, we
do not believe it has fully materialized yet.

Even if confidence in the baseline outlook remains low, a rate hike could
be possible if upside inflation risks materialize significantly or rise
further, while downside growth risks or the risk of a large economic
adjustment remain contained.

---

### Q10. If the Strait of Hormuz remains effectively closed, would that prevent rate hikes?

**A:**

It depends on the outlook for the situation. Even if the Strait of Hormuz
remains closed, a rate hike could be possible depending on how we assess
inflation risks and growth risks based on the expected future path.

The impact on consumer prices may not appear very clearly in the data by
the next meeting. It may show up later. But if the risk of further
inflation rises, we could make decisions based on forecasts without waiting
for all of it to appear in actual data.

---

### Q11. Did government comments affect BOJ independence?

**A:**

The decision to hold rates was made after BOJ discussion and judged
appropriate by us.

We maintain close communication with the government and will continue to do
so, but the decision was made independently by the BOJ.

---

### Q12. Could Japan face a 1970s-style inflation shock?

**A:**

The risk is not very high.

In the early 1970s, before the oil shock, Japan’s economy was already
overheating, and both prices and wages were already rising at high rates.
The oil shock then made the situation much worse.

Today, before the recent worsening in Middle East tensions, Japan’s economy
was not overheating to that extent. Prices were not rising far above 2%,
and wages were not rising far above inflation plus productivity growth.

However, one important initial condition is that the current policy rate is
still below neutral. We need to keep that in mind when conducting policy.

---

### Q13. Are you worried that if policy is too slow now, the terminal rate may need to rise above neutral later?

**A:**

Yes, that is a risk.

If underlying inflation rises clearly and meaningfully above 2%, then we
may later need stronger tightening to bring it back down. In that case, the
terminal rate could potentially exceed neutral, increasing economic
volatility. That is something we need to be careful about.

---

### Q14. If real rates fall further because inflation expectations rise, how does that affect policy?

**A:**

If real rates remain low or fall further, that could support the economy
but also increase upside inflation risks.

We recognize that the policy rate is still somewhat below neutral. Other
things equal, we remain in the process of gradually adjusting the policy
rate toward neutral.

---

### Q15. How does the BOJ view private credit risks?

**A:**

Japan’s exposure to private credit is not very large.

However, the sector is opaque, and we need to monitor it carefully. Since
last summer, there have been some warning signs, and this year, especially
in the first quarter, there were some retail outflows, although within
normal rules.

At this stage, we do not see a systemic situation. Prices of private credit
or riskier corporate credit have not fallen sharply. But there are data
gaps, so we will continue working with overseas authorities to understand
the situation better.

---

### Q16. Has the BOJ used different underlying inflation indicators opportunistically over time?

**A:**

No. Looking at headline CPI, CPI excluding fresh food, CPI excluding energy
and food, trimmed means, and other measures is standard practice among
central banks. These indicators are used to understand underlying inflation
from different angles.

The BOJ is not doing anything unusual in that respect.

---

### Q17. Compared with the March meeting, has concern about upside inflation risk increased?

**A:**

It is difficult to quantify the number of members.

In March, the forecasts were still based on the January Outlook Report, and
some members already emphasized upside inflation risks. This time, we
revised the inflation outlook upward, and some members still pointed to
further upside risks beyond that. In particular, three members judged that
those risks justified a rate hike.

---

### Q18. The Outlook Report removed wording that linked hikes to “improvement” in the economy and prices. Does this mean rate hikes are no longer tied to economic improvement? Is Japan entering stagflation?

**A:**

The typical rate-hike path is still one where confidence in the outlook
rises.

However, even if the economy slows somewhat, as long as there is no major
downside growth shock, rate hikes could still be possible if inflation
follows the projected path or upside inflation risks materialize. In that
sense, the phrase “depending on the degree of improvement in the economy
and prices” no longer fully fits, so we changed the wording.

On stagflation, it depends on the definition. Under our outlook, growth
slows and inflation rises. But both are expected to be temporary, although
the rise in inflation may last somewhat longer.

---

### Q19. Why does core-core CPI remain high in FY2027 even though the baseline assumes Middle East tensions ease and no major supply-chain disruption?

**A:**

The main reason is that higher oil and petrochemical prices are gradually
passed through to various goods and, to some extent, services.

This pass-through proceeds slowly. Inflation is likely to peak around the
first half of FY2027 and then decline, but the annual average for FY2027
remains high because of that timing.

---

### Q20. At the next meeting, the BOJ will review the JGB purchase-reduction plan. Should that be considered together with rate hikes?

**A:**

They should be judged separately.

JGB purchase reductions are about reducing purchases in a predictable
manner while preserving flexibility to ensure market stability.

Policy rates are judged based on economic, price, and financial conditions,
including the likelihood of the outlook and risks. These are separate
decision frameworks.
