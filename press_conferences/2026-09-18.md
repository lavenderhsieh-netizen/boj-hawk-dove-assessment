# BOJ Press Conference — 17–18 Sep 2026

**Decision:** Hike to 1.25%  
**Vote:** 7–2 (Asada, Sato dissented for hold)  
**Presider:** Governor UEDA Kazuo

---

## Core Takeaways

- Policy rate raised 25bp to **1.25%** — the highest level in 31 years (since 1995) — on a 7–2 vote. The two dissenters were **Asada Toichiro and Sato Ayano**, both newly appointed to the Board under the Takaichi administration (Asada from Apr 2026, Sato from Jun 2026); Ueda said their opposition rested mainly on GDP data suggesting domestic demand is not as strong as the majority believes, a weakness the Bank itself is also watching.
- Ueda explicitly declared a **"phase change"** (局面変化) in policy — his strongest framing of this kind to date. With underlying inflation having moved from below 2% to now approaching roughly 2%, the short-term aim of policy shifts from *pushing inflation up* to *anchoring/stabilizing it at around 2%*, requiring the Bank to weigh upside and downside risks **symmetrically** rather than lean toward the downside as it had while inflation sat below target.
- No fixed cadence going forward: Ueda explicitly ruled out any mechanical pace (e.g. "once every three months") and said timing/pace will continue to be set meeting-by-meeting based on the outlook and risk checks — but he also declined to rule out a 50bp move or consecutive hikes if warranted by the price outlook, while stressing a preference to avoid abrupt tightening that destabilizes financial conditions.
- Consumer-level pass-through language was explicitly upgraded from June's "may spread to consumer prices" to "has started spreading to consumer prices," based on the Jun–Aug CPI prints (also released same-day for Aug).
- Risk factors retained: Middle East tensions (crude prices rising again — a "second wave" of pass-through, tied to reported Saudi-related disruption whose duration is highly uncertain), AI-related demand, and FX/yen weakness. All three were again cited as sources of upside price risk, alongside faster-than-usual cost pass-through evidence.
- Declined to discuss the content of his 30 Aug meeting with US Treasury Secretary Bessent, or Bessent's later G20 remarks urging Japan to end reflationary policy; reiterated that BOJ policy accounts for FX's effect on prices but does not target the exchange rate itself. The yen weakened ~¥1.50 immediately after today's hike despite the move being telegraphed — several reporters pressed on whether 25bp was "not enough."
- Fed, ECB and BOJ all hiked in the same month for the first time — Ueda attributed this to shared drivers (Middle East-driven inflation, AI-demand strength affecting both supply and demand sides) rather than coordination.
- Reaffirmed BOJ independence (Article 3/4 framing) when asked about the Takaichi government's "Accord" and its reflationist lineage; declined to comment on fiscal policy directly, but said close communication with the government continues "at various levels" — notably, no pre-meeting Ueda–Takaichi meeting was confirmed this cycle, unlike ahead of the Dec-2025 and Jun-2026 hikes.
- The Climate Response Financing Operation was revised to a floating rate linked to the policy rate with a ¥50tn cap — described as a technical operational tweak (some banks had reportedly been using it as a substitute after pandemic-relief lending operations wound down), not a tightening or easing signal.
- Flagged an unusually long implementation lag (6 days, spanning a holiday) between the rate decision and when it takes effect — Ueda said the Bank would consider this when scheduling future meetings.
- No numerical neutral-rate estimate offered; Ueda repeated that the neutral/terminal rate is a "conceptual variable" difficult to pin down, shaped by the medium-term neutral nominal rate, near-term economic/price judgment, and overseas rates — will only be knowable "after the fact."

## Q&A Transcript

### Q1. Middle East risk reassessment and the quickening pace of hikes (lead reporters, 2 questions)

**Q:** Has the Middle East risk changed since July? Why did the BOJ accelerate the pace of hikes from roughly every six months to only three months this time?

**A:** Uncertainty surrounding the Middle East remains high. Despite the associated downside pressure, Japan's economy continues to recover moderately, supported by AI-related demand and government measures. On prices, oil and other commodity prices have risen again. Higher energy costs could spread to a broader range of consumer prices. Strong AI-related demand and the previous depreciation of the yen could also feed through from business-to-business prices into consumer prices. With underlying inflation approaching 2%, financial conditions still accommodative and upside risks to inflation requiring greater attention, we judged that another rate increase was appropriate. There is no predetermined three-month hiking cycle. We will decide the timing and pace at each meeting based on the outlook and risks. The important change is that the policy regime has shifted. Previously, underlying inflation was below 2%, so the near-term objective was to push it higher. Now that underlying inflation is approaching 2%, the objective is increasingly to keep it anchored around 2% and prevent an overshoot.

---

### Q2. Could the hiking pace keep accelerating, and does a faster pace lift the terminal rate? (Bloomberg — Ito)

**Q:** If the outlook continues to materialize and financial conditions remain accommodative, could the BOJ continue hiking at a faster pace? Does a faster hiking pace imply a higher terminal rate?

**A:** We do not have a specific interval such as "once every three months" in mind. We will assess the economic and inflation outlook and its risks at every meeting. The terminal rate should be considered separately from the pace of hikes. Conceptually, it will depend on the medium- to long-term nominal neutral rate, how far policy needs to deviate from neutral given economic and inflation conditions, and potentially developments in overseas interest rates. The neutral rate is very difficult to identify precisely. Therefore, it is also difficult to identify the terminal rate in advance. We will adjust policy as necessary while observing incoming data, and the terminal rate will effectively become clear ex post.

---

### Q3. Timing of 2% entrenchment; what data matters, incl. next year's shunto (Jiji Press — Aka)

**Q:** How close is underlying inflation to 2%? Could it reach the target sooner than the BOJ's FY2026 H2–FY2027 projection? What data will matter most for future hikes?

**A:** Our basic view remains consistent with the existing outlook: underlying inflation should reach a level broadly consistent with 2% from the second half of FY2026 into FY2027. When I say it is "quite close" to 2%, I mean that if we could summarize underlying inflation with a single indicator, it would probably already be somewhere near 2%. But what matters is not simply touching 2%; we need to determine whether inflation can remain sustainably around 2%, and confirming that will take time. We will continue to monitor the Middle East, commodity prices, exchange rates and other variables affecting the baseline outlook and risks. Wage developments, including the FY2027 Shunto, will also be an important factor in judging whether 2% inflation is becoming entrenched.

---

### Q4. Are past hikes having any effect; today's two dissents; the 2027 board reshuffle (Asahi Shimbun — Sakamoto)

**Q:** Financial conditions still look extremely accommodative. Doesn't that argue for another early hike? What do you make of the two dissenting votes?

**A:** Financial conditions remain accommodative, although the degree of accommodation is gradually diminishing as we raise the policy rate. We look at financial conditions through several channels: interest rates, bank lending, and asset markets including equities and foreign exchange. Long-term and super-long-term interest rates have already tightened considerably. The bank-lending and asset-market channels remain somewhat more accommodative. At the same time, we need to avoid raising rates so rapidly that we trigger an abrupt adjustment in bank lending or asset markets. That potential downside also needs to be considered. Regarding the two dissenters, they placed greater emphasis on signs of weakness in domestic demand, including in GDP data. Members who supported the hike were aware of those weaknesses but placed greater weight on other factors, including the possibility that some weakness was temporary or technical. This was a normal and appropriate policy debate. Future changes in Policy Board membership will not determine the timing or pace of our policy decisions.

---

### Q5. Symmetric risk management going forward (Market News — Inoue)

**Q:** How has the BOJ's approach to risk management changed now that underlying inflation is close to 2%?

**A:** Previously, if upside inflation risks materialized while underlying inflation was still below 2%, the resulting increase in inflation was not necessarily undesirable. That is no longer the case. If underlying inflation is already around 2%, an upside surprise that pushes it materially above 2% becomes a problem. At the same time, downside risks remain important: an unexpected deterioration in the economy could push underlying inflation back down. So policy must now respond more symmetrically to upside and downside risks. Previously, the greater concern about downside risks contributed to a gradual pace of rate increases. Going forward, we will assess both sides more symmetrically when determining the timing and pace of policy changes.

---

### Q6. Bank of Japan independence and the Takaichi government's "Accord" (outlet unclear — reporter Takada)

**Q:** How does the BOJ view its independence and its relationship with the government?

**A:** Close communication with the government is important. Monetary policy cannot be conducted without understanding the direction of fiscal and broader economic policy. But after maintaining close communication and understanding the government's policies, the BOJ must make the monetary-policy decisions it considers appropriate. That is how we understand and apply the BOJ Act.

---

### Q7. Middle East oil, CPI peak timing, and whether 50bp is possible (Reuters — Kihara)

**Q:** How will the latest rise in oil prices affect CPI? Separately, could the BOJ raise rates by more than 25bp, or hike at consecutive meetings?

**A:** Think of the oil effect as potentially coming in two waves. The first wave of oil-related price increases in March and April affected corporate prices and is now gradually feeding through to consumer prices. We are now seeing a possible second wave of increases in oil, fuel and related prices. How persistent this will be is highly uncertain and depends on developments in the Middle East and supply routes. If the increase persists, it could once again lift corporate prices and subsequently feed through into CPI. We need to monitor that carefully. On the size/pace question: it depends on inflation conditions. We cannot rule out specific policy options in advance. A larger hike or repeated consecutive hikes would become relevant if the risk of inflation rising significantly above target became high, or if inflation had already materially overshot the target. The US and Europe in 2022–23 provide examples of such circumstances. Our intention is to make pre-emptive adjustments now so that we can reduce the probability of eventually being forced into very large rate increases that could cause significant disruption to the economy or markets.

---

### Q8. Overseas central-bank hikes and the risk of "waiting" (TV Tokyo — Naka)

**Q:** How do the Fed and ECB's recent rate hikes affect BOJ policy? What is the "cost of waiting" now?

**A:** We have not spoken directly with their governors about the precise reasons for their decisions, but there appear to be some common global forces. These include inflationary pressure stemming from Middle East developments and strong AI-related spending. AI investment may eventually affect the supply side, but so far it has also provided strong demand-side support and some inflationary pressure. Foreign central-bank decisions can affect Japanese inflation through various channels, including financial markets and exchange rates, so we will monitor them carefully. On the cost of waiting: it depends on two things — the risk that underlying inflation rises above the 2% target, and the benefit of waiting in order to obtain and analyze more information. We are currently in a delicate phase that requires careful analysis. But that does not mean moving slowly is necessarily appropriate. We need to analyze developments carefully and then react — including changing policy — at the appropriate time.

---

### Q9. Why September rather than July; would 1.5% still be "accommodative"? (Yomiuri Shimbun — Tanaka)

**Q:** If the economic assessment has not changed dramatically since July, why hike now rather than in July? Separately, if the policy rate rises to 1.5%, would the BOJ still regard financial conditions as accommodative?

**A:** Several things have evolved. First, Middle East developments have moved somewhat more in the direction of creating upside inflation risks. Second, information from overseas policymakers and market participants suggests that the strength of AI-related activity has persisted longer than expected. It is not necessarily accelerating further, but the strength has been sustained. Third, some measures of medium- to long-term inflation expectations rose during the second quarter and have remained elevated. Finally, wage data have remained firm for several months following the Shunto results, giving us greater confidence that the wage developments seen in the spring negotiations are spreading through the broader economy. These developments do not mean underlying inflation is accelerating sharply. Rather, they strengthen our confidence that it is converging toward 2%. On 1.5%: we cannot determine that in advance. If and when the policy rate reaches 1.5%, we will evaluate the economic and financial conditions prevailing at that time and judge whether overall financial conditions remain accommodative.

---

### Q10. Relationship with PM Takaichi ahead of this hike (Nikkei — Takeuchi)

**Q:** Has communication between the BOJ and the Takaichi government changed? Previous hikes were preceded by meetings between Ueda and the prime minister, but this one was not.

**A:** We continue to maintain close communication with the Takaichi administration at various levels.

---

### Q11. A 31-year-high policy rate and the difficulty of the new phase (TBS — Ino)

**Q:** At 1.25%, Japan is moving further into a level of interest rates it has not experienced for decades. What exactly does Ueda mean by a "change in policy phase"?

**A:** The change in phase is not primarily because the policy rate has reached 1.25%. The important change is that underlying inflation has moved from being below 2% to being roughly around 2%. That means policy now has to respond symmetrically to upside and downside inflation risks. Going forward, with underlying inflation around 2%, the natural course would ordinarily be to raise the policy rate toward the neutral rate. But we also need to respond to upside inflation risks. The difficulty is that we have to proceed without knowing precisely where the neutral rate is.

---

### Q12. Can large or consecutive hikes avoid hurting the economy? (Kyodo News — Take)

**Q:** How should we reconcile your desire to avoid abrupt tightening with your refusal to rule out consecutive hikes or a 50bp move?

**A:** Large rate increases or repeated consecutive hikes would generally become necessary if inflation were at serious risk of rising far above target, or had already done so. Our aim is precisely to avoid reaching that situation. By making appropriate pre-emptive adjustments now, we can reduce the likelihood of eventually being forced into very large hikes that could create unexpected disruption in the economy or financial markets.

---

### Q13. JGB purchase tapering and its role in rising long yields (Tokyo Shimbun — Ishii)

**Q:** Is the BOJ's reduction in JGB purchases contributing materially to the rise in long-term yields?

**A:** Supply-demand conditions can affect long-term interest rates, and there is a long history of research on this question. However, I am not aware of recent analysis suggesting that supply-demand factors have been an overwhelmingly large driver of the recent move in long-term yields.

---

### Q14. The Bessent meeting, the yen's reaction, and why not 50bp (outlet unclear)

**Q:** Did US Treasury Secretary Bessent's comments influence the BOJ? Was a 25bp hike insufficient given the yen's reaction?

**A:** I will not comment on the substance of my discussions with Secretary Bessent. The BOJ takes exchange-rate movements into account to the extent that they affect Japan's inflation outlook. But monetary policy is not conducted for the purpose of controlling the exchange rate or keeping the yen within a particular range. Whether we hike consecutively or by a particular amount depends on our assessment of the economic outlook and risks. We cannot commit to such actions in advance.

---

### Q15. Why the pass-through language advanced from June to September (independent — Naoi)

**Q:** What specifically changed in the BOJ's assessment of pass-through from corporate prices to consumer prices?

**A:** Previously, based on various information and historical patterns, we expected higher corporate prices to pass through to consumer prices. We now have CPI data for June, July and August showing that price increases are indeed appearing in the categories where we expected them. There are fluctuations in magnitude, but consumer prices are moving broadly in the direction we anticipated. In that sense, the pass-through is no longer merely prospective; it has begun to materialize.

---

### Q16. Abenomics-era reflation policy and Bessent's G20 remarks (Arc Times — Okada)

**Q:** Has Japan now moved beyond the reflationary policy framework associated with Abenomics?

**A:** For a long period, underlying inflation was below 2%, so monetary policy was aimed at raising it toward the target. Underlying inflation has now moved close to 2%. Therefore, the policy objective going forward shifts from raising underlying inflation toward 2% to keeping it sustainably anchored around 2%.

---

### Q17. The unusually long six-day gap before the hike takes effect (Nikkei — Shimizu)

**Q:** Hikes used to take effect same-day; this cycle they've consistently taken effect two days later, but this time — spanning a longer holiday — implementation is six days out, an unusually long interval with overseas markets open throughout. Could that create any problems?

**A:** Any problem would come from something unexpected; a long gap between decision and effect can, in some circumstances, be undesirable, so the Bank will consider this factor in scheduling future MPMs.

---

### Q18. Are Bessent's concerns about Japanese yields and the yen justified? (freelance — Hara)

**Q:** Bessent has openly voiced concern about Japan's rising long yields and yen weakness. Is that concern justified or overblown? Are 3% 10-year and 4% super-long yields simply normalization, or a sign of an underlying problem?

**A:** It's unclear what's behind Bessent's specific concern about US spillovers. Generally speaking, long-yield levels should be assessed relative to inflation trends and economic strength. [Declined to elaborate further.]

---

### Q19. Was market communication around the Bessent remarks difficult? (Mainichi Shimbun — Takada)

**Q:** Bessent's remarks were widely read as pressure on the BOJ to hike, and pricing for today's move was already well advanced going into the meeting as a result — was there anything you found difficult about market communication in that context?

**A:** Various people's remarks affecting the market is something the Bank can only treat as a factor to anticipate; the Bank continually considers why the market reacted as it did, whether that leaves a medium-term mark on positioning, and whether an unexpectedly large reaction could follow the next policy decision.

---

### Q20. What "phase change" means, and Takata's call for "nimble" hikes (Yomiuri Shimbun — Okamoto)

**Q:** Takata described a "new phase" and argued for agile rate hikes. Is your "change in phase" the same thing? What does it imply for the next move?

**A:** Takata was referring, as I understand it, partly to developments in the United States and the possibility of a shift there from an easing phase toward a tightening phase. My reference to a "change in phase" concerns Japan specifically: underlying inflation has moved from below 2% to around 2%, so the objective is now to stabilize it there. We have only just entered this phase. We will assess it quantitatively in next month's Outlook Report, and determining whether 2% inflation has become firmly established will take time. But we do not need to wait until that entire process is complete before taking the next policy action. Given the upside risks to underlying inflation and the fact that financial conditions remain accommodative, the next move would basically be another rate increase. We will determine its timing and size based on incoming developments.

---

### Q21. Are financial conditions still "too accommodative" after today's hike? (outlet/reporter unclear)

**Q:** After today's hike, are financial conditions still "too accommodative"?

**A:** They remain accommodative, and the degree of accommodation is gradually declining. Whether they are excessively accommodative is much harder to judge. There are lags from the policy rate to financial conditions and then from financial conditions to inflation. For example, a substantial portion of lending is at fixed rates, meaning changes in policy and market rates take time to affect actual borrowing costs. We therefore need to assess financial conditions carefully, including the cumulative effects of previous rate increases.

---

### Q22. Is disagreement among Policy Board members a problem? (outlet/reporter unclear)

**Q:** Is disagreement among Policy Board members a problem?

**A:** No. Differences of opinion are natural. If members never disagreed, there would be little need for discussion. Members should debate, try to narrow their differences, and where disagreement remains, decide by vote. That is a healthy process. I do not regard dissenting votes themselves as a problem.

---

### Q23. Climate Response Financing Operation changes (Toyo Keizai — Kurosaki)

**Q:** Why did the BOJ change the terms of its Climate Response Financing Operations?

**A:** The changes are intended both to ensure smooth monetary operations and to continue providing stable support for climate-related financing. The lending rate will become variable and linked to the policy rate, while limits will be placed on lending. Those limits reflect how the facility has actually been used and what we consider a reasonable level if current usage trends are extrapolated. The objective is not to materially restrict climate-related financing.

---

### Q24. Fed, ECB and BOJ all hiking in the same month (Bloomberg — Fujioka)

**Q:** The Fed, ECB and BOJ have all raised rates in the same month. Is there a common factor?

**A:** Yes, to some extent. A major common factor is inflationary pressure arising from Middle East developments. Another is the strength of AI-related spending. AI investment may eventually expand the supply side of economies, but so far it has also strengthened demand and generated some inflationary pressure. These common forces may help explain why several central banks have moved at roughly the same time.

---

### Q25. What underpins confidence in a currency? (Asahi Shimbun — Yoshioka)

**Q:** What ultimately determines confidence in a currency? Has confidence in the yen improved?

**A:** The most important foundation of confidence in a currency is price stability. What matters is whether the central bank can keep inflation at an appropriate level — or, if inflation temporarily deviates from that level, whether it has a credible strategy for returning it there. Technological developments in payments are also becoming increasingly relevant to confidence in a currency. Exchange rates are a separate matter. Even a highly credible currency can depreciate temporarily if, for example, domestic interest rates need to be lower than those abroad and yield differentials encourage investors to sell that currency.
