# BOJ Press Conference — 17–18 Sep 2026

**Decision:** Hike to 1.25%  
**Vote:** 7–2 (Asada, Sato dissented for hold)  
**Presider:** Governor UEDA Kazuo

---

## Core Takeaways

- Policy rate raised 25bp to **1.25%** — the highest level in 31 years (since 1995) — on a 7–2 vote. The two dissenters were **Asada Toichiro and Sato Ayano**, both newly appointed to the Board under the Takaichi administration (Asada from Apr 2026, Sato from Jun 2026); Ueda said their opposition rested mainly on GDP data suggesting domestic demand is not as strong as the majority believes, a weakness the Bank itself is also watching.
- Ueda explicitly declared a **"phase change"** (局面変化) in policy — his strongest framing of this kind to date. With underlying inflation having moved from below 2% to now approaching roughly 2%, the short-term aim of policy shifts from *pushing inflation up* to *anchoring/stabilizing it at around 2%*, requiring the Bank to weigh upside and downside risks **symmetrically** rather than lean toward the downside as it had while inflation sat below target.
- No fixed cadence going forward: Ueda explicitly ruled out any mechanical pace (e.g. "once every three months") and said timing/pace will continue to be set meeting-by-meeting based on the outlook and risk checks — but he also declined to rule out a 50bp move or consecutive hikes if warranted by the price outlook, while stressing a preference to avoid abrupt tightening that destabilizes financial conditions.
- Consumer-level pass-through language was explicitly upgraded from June's "may spread to consumer prices" to "has started spreading to consumer prices," based on the Jun–Aug CPI prints (also released same-day for Aug).
- Risk factors retained: Middle East tensions (crude prices rising again — a "second wave" of pass-through, tied to reported Saudi-related disruption whose duration is highly uncertain), AI-related demand, and FX/yen weakness. All three were again cited as sources of upside price risk, alongside faster-than-usual cost pass-through evidence.
- Declined to discuss the content of his 30 Aug meeting with US Treasury Secretary Bessent, or Bessent's later G20 remarks urging Japan to end reflationary policy; reiterated that BOJ policy accounts for FX's effect on prices but does not target the exchange rate itself. The yen weakened ~¥1.50 immediately after today's hike despite the move being telegraphed — several reporters pressed on whether 25bp was "not enough."
- Fed, ECB and BOJ all hiked in the same month for the first time — Ueda attributed this to shared drivers (Middle East-driven inflation, AI-demand strength affecting both supply and demand sides) rather than coordination.
- Reaffirmed BOJ independence (Article 3/4 framing) when asked about the Takaichi government's "Accord" and its reflationist lineage; declined to comment on fiscal policy directly, but said close communication with the government continues "at various levels" — notably, no pre-meeting Ueda–Takaichi meeting was confirmed this cycle, unlike ahead of the Dec-2025 and Jun-2026 hikes.
- The Climate Response Financing Operation was revised to a floating rate linked to the policy rate with a ¥50tn cap — described as a technical operational tweak (some banks had reportedly been using it as a substitute after pandemic-relief lending operations wound down), not a tightening or easing signal.
- Flagged an unusually long implementation lag (6 days, spanning a holiday) between the rate decision and when it takes effect — Ueda said the Bank would consider this when scheduling future meetings.
- No numerical neutral-rate estimate offered; Ueda repeated that the neutral/terminal rate is a "conceptual variable" difficult to pin down, shaped by the medium-term neutral nominal rate, near-term economic/price judgment, and overseas rates — will only be knowable "after the fact."

## Q&A Transcript

### Q1. Middle East risk reassessment and the quickening pace of hikes (lead reporters, 2 questions)

**Q:** The July Outlook Report listed the Middle East as a risk factor but judged large downside/upside risks had diminished — has that changed, and how did it feed into today's decision? Separately, since exiting negative rates the market has priced roughly semiannual hikes; this year's hike came just 3 months after June. Was the pace brought forward because inflation risk was judged to be accelerating, and what's the outlook for pace from here?

**A:** The Middle East situation remains unresolved and still needs watching, but despite the downward pressure it creates, the economy is recovering moderately, supported by AI-related demand and government policy. On prices, crude has turned back up and price pressure from it is likely to keep broadening across items; AI-related demand and past yen weakness are also feeding through into consumer prices via corporate cost pass-through. With the economy and prices tracking the central outlook and underlying inflation approaching 2%, the Bank judged a hike appropriate, bearing in mind these upside risks. On pace: financial conditions remain accommodative, corporate goods prices are running high year-on-year, CPI's positive momentum has widened gradually, and medium-/long-term inflation expectations keep rising — with underlying inflation now near 2%, the priority has shifted from raising it to keeping it stable there without letting it overshoot and later damage the economy. That is the sense in which "the phase of policy has changed."

---

### Q2. Could the hiking pace keep accelerating, and does a faster pace lift the terminal rate? (Bloomberg — Ito)

**Q:** Given the phase change, if the economy keeps normalizing toward 2% while conditions stay accommodative, could the pace continue running faster than the prior roughly six-month cadence — e.g., every three months? Separately, market-implied terminal rates rose alongside speculation of a faster pace — should the pace and the terminal rate be thought of together, or separately?

**A:** The short-term aim of policy has changed considerably, but there is no specific cadence such as "once every three months" in mind — timing and pace will be judged meeting by meeting based on the outlook and risk checks needed to anchor underlying inflation at 2%. On the terminal rate: it's shaped by the medium- to long-term neutral nominal rate, by how far it's appropriate to deviate from it given near-term conditions, and by overseas rates — but the neutral rate itself is a genuinely hard-to-pin-down conceptual variable, so what the terminal rate should be is equally hard to specify in advance; in practice it will only become clear after the fact, as policy is adjusted meeting by meeting.

---

### Q3. Timing of 2% entrenchment; what data matters, incl. next year's shunto (Jiji Press — Aka)

**Q:** You described underlying inflation as "quite close" to 2% at this month's G20 presser. The Bank's own forecast has it reaching a level broadly consistent with 2% from H2 FY2026 into FY2027 — does it now look achievable sooner, even next month, or still more like FY2027? Separately, what data will matter most for judging future hikes, and how important is next year's shunto?

**A:** The current picture is broadly in line with the existing forecast — 2% reached from H2 FY2026 into FY2027. If reduced to one indicator, underlying inflation is now roughly near 2%, but confirming it stays anchored there will take time. On data: the same risk factors (Middle East, yen weakness, FX volatility) remain central to tracking the outlook and its risks; next year's (FY2027) shunto is also very much something the Bank is watching as one key input into whether 2% becomes entrenched.

---

### Q4. Are past hikes having any effect; today's two dissents; the 2027 board reshuffle (Asahi Shimbun — Sakamoto)

**Q:** Bank lending is growing at a high year-on-year pace and financial conditions look extremely accommodative — do past hikes appear to be having little effect? Is an early additional hike needed to correct that before an overshoot risk materializes? Separately, Board members Asada and Sato — both appointed under the Takaichi administration — dissented today; what's your reaction? With hawks Takata and Tamura retiring next July, is there any sense of needing to complete necessary hikes before then?

**A:** Conditions remain accommodative overall, but the degree of accommodation is narrowing gradually as the policy rate rises. Looking at financial conditions across three channels — interest rates, bank lending, and asset markets/FX — long and super-long yields have tightened considerably, while the other two channels remain somewhat accommodative; the Bank will keep watching this, while also being wary of tightening too abruptly and causing a disruptive adjustment in lending or asset prices. On the dissents: Asada and Sato appear to have based their opposition on GDP data suggesting domestic demand is weaker than the majority view — a weakness the Bank is also watching — while majority voters judged some of that apparent weakness to reflect temporary/technical factors; a proper debate, not a rupture. On next July's reshuffle: the Bank has no way to anticipate future appointees and does not intend to let that uncertainty influence the pace of policy between now and then.

---

### Q5. Symmetric risk management going forward (Market News — Inoue)

**Q:** With macro tightening/slowdown not yet clearly visible, but underlying/trend/composite expected inflation all near 2%, how do you see the size and timing of coming hikes from a risk-management perspective?

**A:** There are both upside and downside risks now. Previously, if underlying inflation ended up overshooting while risk wasn't the focus, that was tolerable since it was still rising from below 2%; going forward that would not be acceptable — but an unexpected downside shock dragging underlying inflation down would equally be a problem. So risk management becomes symmetric: where the previous gradual pace reflected more weight on downside risk, future timing and pace will weigh both sides evenly.

---

### Q6. Bank of Japan independence and the Takaichi government's "Accord" (outlet unclear — reporter Takada)

**Q:** Finance Minister Katayama has linked the current BOJ Act's Article 3 (independence) and Article 4 (monetary policy as part of economic policy) to lessons from Japan's past, and said PM Takaichi — close to Abe — carries on that thinking via the government's "Accord." Do the Bank's communications and hikes since June/July, including today's, align with that thinking? With about 18 months left in your term, how do you view BOJ independence now?

**A:** Close communication with the government — whether or not codified in law — is essential everywhere; the Bank cannot set monetary policy without knowing the direction of fiscal policy. Article 3's intent is that the Bank confirms such context through close dialogue and then decides monetary policy on its own judgment — that is how the Bank has operated and will continue to operate.

---

### Q7. Middle East oil, CPI peak timing, and whether 50bp is possible (Reuters — Kihara)

**Q:** With a "second wave" of oil-related price pressure now underway, how does that affect the timing of core CPI's peak, and could an above-2% core CPI period last longer than envisaged in July, heading into the October Outlook Report? Separately, given your caution about abrupt tightening, would a 50bp hike or consecutive hikes be considered, or is that off the table?

**A:** If March–April's oil-driven price rise was a first wave now fading into CPI, a second wave of fuel-related price rises is now underway; how long it persists depends on the uncertain duration of the underlying Saudi-related disruption — a pipeline reopening or alternate routes could shorten it, but a prolonged episode could again feed through corporate goods prices into CPI, and the Bank will watch for that. On a hike above 25bp: in short, it depends entirely on the price situation, and no specific approach is ruled out in advance.

### Q8. Overseas central-bank hikes and the risk of "waiting" (TV Tokyo — Naka)

**Q:** Board Member Takata recently cited overseas central banks pivoting to hikes as a factor behind the phase change; the ECB and Fed have both hiked recently. How do overseas hikes affect BOJ timing/pace, and does a narrower rate gap make it harder to correct yen weakness? Separately, a Board member's Summary-of-Opinions remark — echoing your own past phrasing — said the risk of "waiting" was not small. How do you assess that risk now?

**A:** Without having spoken to the ECB/Fed chiefs post-hike, their exact reasoning is unclear, but possible shared drivers include Middle East-driven inflation and AI-demand strength — both already incorporated into the Bank's own risk assessment; still, overseas moves affect Japanese prices through several channels and warrant attention. On waiting: the cost/benefit depends partly on how large the risk of an inflation overshoot is, and partly on the fact that "waiting" means continuing to analyze data, not simply doing nothing — how much careful analysis is warranted depends on how delicate the juncture is. The Bank sees itself at a juncture requiring careful analysis, but that doesn't mean slowness is fine — it calls for responding, or changing policy, at the appropriate time.

### Q9. Why September rather than July; would 1.5% still be "accommodative"? (Yomiuri Shimbun — Tanaka)

**Q:** The stated assessment looks largely unchanged since July, when a hike would have meant back-to-back moves — what specifically changed to make September the moment, rather than July? Separately, at 1.25% conditions are still called accommodative — if the rate reaches 1.5%, would that still be true, given how hard the neutral rate is to pin down?

**A:** Since July, the Middle East situation has, if anything, worsened toward the upside-risk direction; AI-related strength has persisted longer than expected in conversations with overseas practitioners; some medium-/long-term inflation-expectation indicators rose and have held; and wage data reflecting this year's shunto have stayed solid for several months, increasingly confirming that the shunto outcome is spreading economy-wide — together pointing to underlying inflation gradually converging toward 2%. On 1.5%: a formulaic but honest answer — that judgment would be made at the time, based on economic and financial developments between now and then.

**Q:** Ahead of the December 2025 and June 2026 hikes you met directly with PM Takaichi about a month prior; that didn't happen this time. Does that reflect a change in communication style, or a shift in the administration's (reportedly hike-cautious) thinking?

**A:** The Bank continues close communication with the Takaichi administration at various levels. [No further detail offered.]

### Q11. A 31-year-high policy rate and the difficulty of the new phase (TBS — Ino)

**Q:** The rate is now at its highest in 31 years, taking Japan deeper into unfamiliar territory just as you describe a phase change. What's the difficulty of continuing to hike toward an appropriate rate level while pursuing price stability, and what philosophy should a central bank bring to this kind of transition?

**A:** The phase change is really about underlying inflation moving from below 2% to near 2% — requiring symmetric responses to upside/downside risk — more than about the rate level itself. Going forward, the natural path is to carefully raise the rate toward the neutral rate given inflation near 2%, while still managing upside risk along the way — made harder by not knowing exactly where the neutral rate lies.

### Q12. Can large or consecutive hikes avoid hurting the economy? (Kyodo News — Take)

**Q:** You said you wouldn't rule out consecutive hikes or 50bp moves while also wanting to avoid abrupt tightening — does that mean such a path could be pursued without economic cost, or would some cost be accepted? How likely do you currently see consecutive hikes?

**A:** Large or repeated hikes would depend on whether there's high risk of inflation shooting well past target, or it already having done so — as in the US/Europe in 2022–23. The Bank's approach is to think carefully now and make pre-emptive adjustments so that scenario doesn't arise; underlying inflation is not currently judged to have clearly exceeded target. Acting pre-emptively reduces the risk of being forced later into a large, disruptive hike that shocks the economy or markets.

### Q13. JGB purchase tapering and its role in rising long yields (Tokyo Shimbun — Ishii)

**Q:** As the Bank shrinks its JGB holdings while the market absorbs heavier issuance, is that a contributing factor to the recent rise in long-term yields?

**A:** There's long-standing analysis on how pre-equilibrium supply/demand dynamics affect long yields, and there continues to be some now — but nothing recent suggesting supply/demand factors have had an outsized effect on recent long-yield moves comes to mind.

### Q14. The Bessent meeting, the yen's reaction, and why not 50bp (outlet unclear)

**Q:** Following your 30 Aug meeting with Treasury Secretary Bessent, the US Treasury reportedly signaled Japan should take resolute monetary action against excessive yen weakness — arguably a fair point. Yet after today's 25bp hike, the yen weakened roughly ¥1.50 almost immediately, having barely strengthened even on advance reports of a hike. Doesn't that suggest 25bp wasn't enough? Why not 50bp, and why the continued caution on consecutive hikes?

**A:** No comment on the content of the Bessent meeting. Japan's monetary policy takes account of FX's effect on domestic prices when setting rates, but it is not conducted to control or stabilize the exchange rate within any particular range. Whether consecutive hikes occur depends on the evolving economic outlook and risk assessment — not something that can be promised in advance.

### Q15. Why the pass-through language advanced from June to September (independent — Naoi)

**Q:** In June, the statement said cost pass-through "may spread" to consumer prices; today's language says it "has started spreading" — a notably more advanced description. What data specifically justifies that shift?

**A:** Pass-through from corporate to consumer prices had been anticipated based on past patterns; the June, July and (released today) August national CPI data show that, for the items expected to see pass-through, this is now occurring to some degree — with some variation in magnitude, but broadly moving in the anticipated direction.

### Q16. Abenomics-era reflation policy and Bessent's G20 remarks (Arc Times — Okada)

**Q:** Secretary Bessent reportedly told the G20 press corps he'd told Japan to "stop" its reflationary policy after the "success" of Abenomics. Given you told me in December we were at a "winding-down" stage for that policy, how do you now see the future of Japan's fiscal and monetary policy stance, building on the reflationist era?

**A:** No direct comment on fiscal policy, as a central bank. On monetary policy: for a long period the aim was raising underlying inflation toward the sustainable 2% goal, since it hadn't been reached; now that it is roughly near 2%, the aim shifts to anchoring it there.

### Q17. The unusually long six-day gap before the hike takes effect (Nikkei — Shimizu)

**Q:** Hikes used to take effect same-day; this cycle they've consistently taken effect two days later, but this time — spanning a longer holiday — implementation is six days out, an unusually long interval with overseas markets open throughout. Could that create any problems?

**A:** Any problem would come from something unexpected; a long gap between decision and effect can, in some circumstances, be undesirable, so the Bank will consider this factor in scheduling future MPMs.

### Q20. What "phase change" means, and Takata's call for "nimble" hikes (Yomiuri Shimbun — Okamoto)

**Q:** You haven't used language as strong as "phase change" before. What does it mean for the pace of hikes? Board Member Takata recently spoke of a "new phase" requiring nimble hikes — do you share that view?

**A:** Takata's phrasing likely refers to overseas central banks possibly shifting from an easing to a hiking phase given economic strength. My own use of "phase change" refers to Japan's underlying inflation moving from below 2% toward roughly 2%, making anchoring it there the new policy aim. This is only the threshold of that shift — next month's Outlook Report will offer a more quantitative read, and confirming entrenchment will take time, though policy needn't wait for full confirmation before acting. For now, given the accommodative backdrop and the overshoot risk being flagged, the next step is basically a further hike, with pace and size to be judged as conditions develop.

### Q21. Climate Response Financing Operation changes (Toyo Keizai — Kurosaki)

**Q:** The operation was changed to a floating rate with a ¥50 trillion cap; some banks reportedly used it as a substitute after pandemic-relief lending programs wound down. What's the thinking behind the change, the ¥50tn figure, and could it change further?

**A:** The revision balances smooth money-market operations with stable support for climate response, hence linking the lending rate to the policy rate. The ¥50tn cap reflects a reasonable ceiling given usage trends to date if extended forward — not an attempt to significantly brake climate support or monetary easing.

### Q22. Fed, ECB and BOJ all hiking in the same month (Bloomberg — Fujioka)

**Q:** This is reportedly the first time the Fed, ECB and BOJ have all hiked in the same month. What's your read on this coincidence?

**A:** Likely reflects shared drivers — Middle East-driven inflation pressure and strong AI-related spending, which so far has mainly boosted demand (and some inflation) rather than supply, across many countries — a common factor behind several central banks moving around the same time, alongside country-specific nuances.

### Q23. What underpins confidence in a currency? (Asahi Shimbun — Yoshioka)

**Q:** Probably the last question of a landmark meeting — what, in your view, secures confidence in a currency? After three and a half years as Governor, has confidence in the yen strengthened? What does a central bank need to do, near-term (this year) and long-term, to strengthen that confidence?

**A:** The core, long-standing determinant of currency confidence is whether prices are stable in the country that issues it — whether the central bank keeps prices/inflation at an appropriate level, or has a clear strategy to return to it after any temporary deviation. Payment-technology developments are becoming a growing secondary factor. The exchange rate is somewhat separate: even a high-confidence currency can be sold on yield-differential grounds if its interest rate needs to be temporarily low for domestic reasons.
