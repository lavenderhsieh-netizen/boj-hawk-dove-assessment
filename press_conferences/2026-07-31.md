# BOJ Press Conference — 30–31 Jul 2026

**Decision:** Hold at 1.00%  
**Vote:** 8–1 (Takata dissented for a hike to 1.25%)  
**Presider:** Governor UEDA Kazuo *(back from illness)*

---

## Core Takeaways

- Policy rate held at 1.00% (8–1); Takata dissented for the fifth consecutive meeting, proposing a hike to 1.25% on upside price risks from overseas supply/demand shocks and shifting global financial conditions.
- Two alternative-language proposals were also defeated: Takata's wording that CPI inflation had "already broadly reached" the price-stability target, and Tamura's wording that underlying inflation was "moving at a level consistent with" the target — the Board kept its own more cautious phrasing ("approaching 2%").
- Governor Ueda chaired after returning from illness ("I have recovered and am carrying out my duties as usual"); new Board member Sato voted with the majority in his first meeting.
- The July Outlook trimmed the FY2026 core-CPI forecast — mechanical, driven by the government's summer energy-relief subsidies, not a softening of the trend; the core-core (ex-fresh-food-and-energy) path is broadly unchanged.
- Risk balance: economic-activity risk upgraded from "skewed to the downside" (April) to "generally balanced"; price risk stays skewed to the upside, with the overshoot-above-2% warning retained.
- The list of risk factors the Bank will weigh on timing/pace was expanded from one to three: Middle East developments, AI-related demand, and exchange-rate movements. Ueda declined to say this signals a faster pace — "that depends on how those factors develop."
- Forward guidance kept intact: the Bank "expects to continue raising the policy rate and adjusting the degree of monetary accommodation," and explicitly wants to avoid falling behind the curve.
- Opened with condolences for the 2026 Kumamoto earthquake; still assessing economic impact (concentration of semiconductor/transport-equipment plants, logistics damage) but sees no major disruption to financial functions or settlement so far.
- New fiscal variable flagged: a planned two-year cut to the consumption tax on food/beverages from April 2027 — Ueda called the CPI impact uncertain (cites mixed European experience) and too early to quantify.
- AI-driven demand continues to show up as a two-sided price risk: strong, seemingly persistent capex is pushing up memory/semiconductor prices now, but a future shortfall in AI investment returns could remove that pressure or trigger an asset-price adjustment.

## Q&A Transcript

### Q1. Effects of the June rate hike and central-bank independence (Sankei Shimbun)

**Q:** What effects has the June hike had on the economy? Markets expect another increase this year — how do you view further hikes? How does the Bank view its distance from the government given the Basic Policy episode that triggered yen/JGB selling?

**A:** Market and lending rates have risen since June, including short-term prime and deposit rates from August, but financial conditions remain accommodative — corporate fund demand and lending attitudes stay active, CP/corporate-bond issuance conditions are favourable, and no conspicuous effect on sentiment has emerged yet. Policy effects take time to filter through, so the Bank will keep examining the impact of past hikes. With underlying inflation approaching 2% and conditions still accommodative, the Bank expects to keep raising rates and adjusting accommodation, deciding timing/pace after assessing the central outlook and the Middle East/AI/FX risks. On the government's Basic Policy, the Bank reads it as setting fiscal/growth direction while expressing hopes for BOJ policy — but the Bank will keep conducting policy under its own judgment. Delaying necessary tightening while upside risks are high could ultimately destabilise markets and the economy.

---

### Q2. Judging whether 2% inflation is entrenched (Reuters)

**Q:** Does highlighting Middle East/AI/FX/upside risks signal conditions for another hike may soon be in place? How will the Bank judge entrenchment — does it need next spring's wage round first?

**A:** These are the three factors the Bank currently views as most important; all will be weighed at future meetings alongside other data. There's no single indicator of entrenchment — the Bank looks at whether many underlying-inflation measures are converging near 2% and whether the reasons behind that look durable. It would also be evidence of entrenchment if headline inflation swings with shocks while underlying measures stay stable near 2%. Perfect entrenchment does not need to be confirmed before acting.

---

### Q3. September timing and falling behind the curve (Bloomberg)

**Q:** Does stronger concern about upside inflation put a September move in play? Markets still fear the Bank falling behind the curve and further yen weakness.

**A:** The Bank will keep analysing the relevant factors and discuss policy thoroughly at coming meetings. Underlying inflation is judged to be moving quite close to 2%, and the risk of a further rise cannot be ignored — policy will be managed so this does not happen in a harmful way, i.e. so the Bank does not fall behind the curve.

---

### Q4. Food consumption-tax cut and flexible bond purchases (Jiji Press)

**Q:** The government plans a two-year food/beverage consumption-tax cut from April 2027 — what's the CPI and fiscal effect? Under what conditions would the Bank flexibly step up JGB purchases if long yields spike?

**A:** European experience with similar measures shows the CPI effect doesn't mechanically match the tax change — pass-through to food prices is uncertain, and the temporary nature could distort the timing of household purchases around the cut and its 2029 reversal. Too early for a numerical estimate; the Bank will analyse once details are finalised. It won't comment on funding sources, only that market confidence in medium-term fiscal soundness matters. On bond purchases, the Bank won't pre-announce precise trigger conditions — it would make a comprehensive judgment at the time, looking at things like an exceptionally rapid yield rise or a serious market-functioning problem.

---

### Q5. Probability of an inflation overshoot; bankruptcies (TBS)

**Q:** How likely is an overshoot above 2%? Does the Bank need to act more flexibly? Are rising bankruptcies a healthy form of renewal?

**A:** No specific probability has been produced, but the possibility is too significant to ignore; the number of Board members flagging upside CPI risk gives some signal, though that refers to headline CPI rather than underlying inflation specifically. Bankruptcies are rising mainly on labour shortages and cost pressure at SMEs, not high interest payments. Painful for those involved, but if displaced workers reallocate to firms that use them better, it can benefit the broader economy.

---

### Q6. Lagged effects of hikes and the Kumamoto earthquake (Yomiuri Shimbun)

**Q:** How long before the Bank assesses hike effects and considers another move — what data matters most? What is the economic impact of the Kumamoto earthquake?

**A:** Monetary transmission can take 1.5–2 years, so the Bank keeps examining lagged effects of past hikes. New lending rates adjust quickly, but existing loan stock — especially floating-rate mortgages under Japan's five-year/125% rules — repricing is slow, so some effects on older mortgages may only now be emerging. On Kumamoto: too early for a full assessment; some bank branches/ATMs closed but no major disruption to settlement or cash supply seen, and the Bank has asked institutions to facilitate fund supply. The region concentrates semiconductor and transport-equipment plants, so the impact depends on how long production stoppages last and logistics damage.

---

### Q7. Consistency with government growth policy; role of FX (Nikkei)

**Q:** Are the hikes needed for price stability consistent with the government's growth strategy? Has FX pass-through to prices strengthened, and does yen weakness argue for a faster pace?

**A:** Under Article 2 of the BOJ Act, contributing to sound economic development runs through price stability — letting inflation run and then having to hike abruptly later would be more disruptive to markets and investment. Business interviews cite labour shortages and material costs, not high rates, as the main investment constraint, reinforcing that price stability itself supports investment. FX pass-through to prices does look somewhat stronger than in the past, and with underlying inflation approaching 2% the upside risk from a weaker yen matters more than when inflation was far below target — but FX remains an input to the price outlook, not a target in itself.

---

### Q8. Has tightening had any effect, and could pace accelerate? (Market News)

**Q:** If past hikes haven't visibly constrained the economy and financing costs stay low, does that imply a higher neutral rate and a case for hiking faster?

**A:** Yields beyond the very short end have risen materially, partly reflecting past hikes, and rates around the three-year area are likely most relevant to spending — financial conditions need to be assessed comprehensively. The Bank cannot state precisely how far it is from neutral; if it judges conditions to be excessively accommodative it could hike faster.

---

### Q9. JGB market functioning three years after the YCC shift (Nikkin)

**Q:** Has market functioning and price signalling recovered as expected since the Bank ceded more influence to the market on long rates?

**A:** Functioning has recovered to some degree, broadly as expected — trading volume is up, and bargain-buying appears when yields jump sharply, suggesting markets reflect fundamentals reasonably well, even if the recovery isn't complete.

---

### Q10. AI demand, inflation persistence, and downside spending risk (Kyodo News)

**Q:** How persistent is AI-driven inflation, and could weak AI investment returns slow the broader economy?

**A:** AI demand is very strong and has pushed memory prices up sharply — a real inflation driver and a source of further upside risk, with persistence depending on how durable AI demand proves. Despite a recent equity correction, capex data and company interviews suggest AI spending has meaningful staying power, which matters for whether these price rises feed into underlying inflation. The risk that this exceptional spending growth eventually stops must still be monitored.

---

### Q11. Deflation-return risk and the Governor's health (Mainichi Shimbun)

**Q:** The PM recently said a return to deflation remains possible, versus the Bank's upside-risk framing — do you see deflation risk? How is your health?

**A:** The probability of returning to deflation is never literally zero, but it has declined meaningfully from the past. On health: recovered and carrying out duties as usual, apologising for the concern caused.

---

### Q12. Japan's policy stance versus Western central banks (TV Tokyo)

**Q:** What risk comes from the Bank staying accommodative while Western central banks may be moving toward hikes?

**A:** Each central bank sets policy independently on its own cycle — the BOJ has been hiking for a while as the US/Europe had generally been cutting; more recently, partly due to the Middle East, Europe has shifted and the US is reportedly weighing a change too.

---

### Q13. AI as a historic technological shift (Asahi Shimbun)

**Q:** Is AI one of history's largest tech shifts from a policy standpoint? The Outlook warns of asset-price/adjustment pressure if returns disappoint — how would that hit the economy and prices?

**A:** AI capex is rising fast and is inflationary in itself. If returns prove insufficient and spending later falls, that inflationary pressure would fade — not yet knowable which path plays out. If adoption succeeds, early spending is inflationary (plus a wealth effect from higher equities), while later, once investment raises productive capacity, it could turn disinflationary — a much longer and more uncertain horizon to track.

---

### Q14. Could yield-curve control return? (Freelance journalist)

**Q:** Is YCC still in the toolkit, and could a central bank really control long rates?

**A:** YCC remains a theoretical tool but is not considered likely to be used again in the near future. A central bank can pin long yields if it commits to unlimited purchases at a chosen price, but that carries significant side effects.

---

### Q15. The Governor's role during the June meeting he missed (outlet unclear)

**Q:** You were absent from June, didn't vote or join remotely, yet a major hike was decided — were you involved in preparing it? Who led it, and was Uchida's presser assignment on your instruction?

**A:** Ueda submitted only a written opinion that day but was involved in Executive-level discussions during preparation (no further detail disclosed). Succession during his absence follows Board-set arrangements and internal rules established when he took office; June's press conference followed those same arrangements.

---

### Q16. Could upside risk pull hikes forward? (Bloomberg)

**Q:** Versus hiking only once the central outlook materialises, could rising upside risk mean hikes come sooner or faster?

**A:** Ultimately depends on the Bank's judgment of how likely those risks are to actually materialise.

---

### Q17. Tokyo CPI and upstream cost pass-through (Reuters)

**Q:** How do you read July's Tokyo CPI? Is raw-material cost pass-through tracking expectations? Will the Bank lean more on upstream indicators (import/corporate-goods prices) to judge overshoot risk?

**A:** Higher raw-material costs are already visible in corporate-goods prices and are expected to keep feeding into retail/consumer prices, with some signs of faster-than-usual pass-through supporting the upside-risk view. The Tokyo CPI print landed mid-meeting so wasn't fully examined, but the initial read is consistent with cost pass-through gradually showing up in consumer prices from July into autumn.

---

### Q18. Meaning of expanding the risk-factor list from one to three (outlet unclear)

**Q:** Should tripling the listed risk factors (Middle East, AI, FX) be read as signalling a faster pace?

**A:** Depends on how those three factors actually develop — they were listed because they could move in ways that affect the policy path, not as a pre-set signal.

---

### Q19. Fiscal expansion versus monetary tightening (Arc Times)

**Q:** The Bank warns of upside inflation and possible faster hikes while the government keeps expansionary fiscal policy — does fiscal policy need to adjust, or must the Bank hike faster to compensate?

**A:** Not the Bank's role to direct fiscal policy; the government describes its stance as "responsible, proactive fiscal policy," which Ueda understands to include some regard for fiscal discipline and the debt-to-GDP ratio.

---

### Q20. Household mortgage risk from longer, larger loans (independent outlet)

**Q:** Lending attitudes toward households look weaker; floating-rate, longer-tenor and joint mortgages are more common — how is the Bank assessing this risk?

**A:** The Bank always checks loan size/maturity against borrower income sustainability. The lending survey has softened, but that's also seasonal for this time of year — the Bank will watch to see if it's a genuine trend.

---

### Q21. Has the overshoot risk kept rising since April/June? (Yomiuri Shimbun)

**Q:** The above-2% overshoot risk was flagged in both April and June — has it kept increasing, and is it likely to rise further?

**A:** Several developments over the past couple of months have pushed in that direction, with medium- to long-term inflation expectations a particular recent focus — several released indicators have risen substantially, and because they're closely tied to underlying inflation the Bank is watching them closely.

---

### Q22. Can Japan ever be formally declared out of deflation? (Toyo Keizai)

**Q:** You said deflation-return risk is never literally zero, yet the government's exit definition requires no prospect of returning to it — does that mean Japan can never formally exit?

**A:** That's a question for the government; Ueda expects that in practice it would judge an exit once the return-probability had fallen below some sufficiently low threshold, not require it to be literally zero.

---

### Q23. Divergence in Board members' inflation forecasts (Nikkei)

**Q:** The spread of Board members' FY2026/27 CPI forecasts has widened versus April — why, and how will consensus be built? Why didn't you vote at the June meeting?

**A:** Some members with higher forecasts also see upside risk, while at least one has a somewhat lower forecast; the Bank is aware of the spread and will work to converge the discussion at future meetings. On June: Ueda was absent and so did not vote, submitting his opinion in writing per the applicable arrangements.
